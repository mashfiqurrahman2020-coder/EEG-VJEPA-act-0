1 Abstract
EEG-VJEPA is a published self-supervised model that adapts video-style JEPA pre-training to EEG, reporting strong results on the TUH Abnormal EEG Corpus (TUAB) [1]. We set out to reproduce it and then build on it. The reproduction failed. The released, pre-trained checkpoints are representation-collapsed: on two completely different datasets, almost every recording we fed in came out as nearly the same output vector, so a classifier built on top of it does no better than one built on random, untrained weights. The collapse is not explained by any preprocessing variation we tested and appears to be a property of the released weights. We followed the paper's own pipeline as closely as its text and code allow (43 of 48 files byte-identical to the authors' public repository) and still could not reach their reported accuracy.
We built our own two-branch alternative in response: FEI (Frequency-masked Embedding Inference), a 1-D convolutional encoder pre-trained to predict, from an intact EEG window, how a slowly-updated copy of itself embeds the same window with random frequency bins removed, and GTJ (GRU-based Time-JEPA), a spectrogram encoder pre-trained by predicting the future course of a recording's spectrum from its past. Fused together (FEI+GTJ), our original pre-training run reaches an AUROC of 0.865 ± 0.024 on five-fold cross-validation on the NMT dataset (2,417 recordings). Averaged over that run and two independently seeded re-runs it scores 0.861, ahead of a classical hand-crafted band-power baseline scored on the identical folds (0.786 ± 0.009; better in all 5 folds, Holm-corrected p = 0.029). An untrained twin of the same fused architecture, averaged over five random seeds, already reaches 0.834, though, so most of that margin comes from the architecture: pre-training adds +0.027 AUROC (0.861 against 0.834), better in all 5 folds but not statistically significant after correction. On a completely different hospital's data (TUAB), FEI pre-trained on NMT alone, with no TUAB data at all, scored +0.059 AUROC above a random-weight twin (p = 0.002); two independently seeded re-runs of that pre-training gave +0.046 and +0.052 (p = 0.007 and 0.004). GTJ did not survive that zero-shot shift (0.840 vs 0.863 for its random twin), so we also re-pre-trained both encoders on NMT plus TUAB's unlabelled training recordings: the fused model then scored close to the released ViT-M checkpoint under our linear probe (0.862 vs 0.838, difference not significant) and beat band power (0.757), but did no better than its own random-weight twin (0.861). We then took the NMT-only encoders to a third, unrelated task: telling stroke patients apart from healthy controls in a small resting-state EEG cohort. There, after a spectrum-matching step we designed post hoc on this same cohort (the input fixed in advance gave 0.833), FEI+GTJ scored close to the clinical Brain Symmetry Index (BSI) baseline (0.926 vs 0.907 AUROC, leave-one-subject-out: a model is validated on one subject it never saw any data from, repeated once per subject). With 15 subjects that difference is far too uncertain to show either superiority or equivalence.
Every pre-trained encoder in this report is checked against a random-initialisation control: the same architecture, untrained weights, same pipeline (one disclosed exception: the released ViT-B in Experiment 1). That habit is what caught the released model's collapse in the first place, and what later caught our own GTJ quietly collapsing under a spectrum shift between datasets, which we then repaired, post hoc and on that same cohort, with a label-free spectral-matching step. FEI over-flags healthy children; GTJ is fragile under distribution shift. Those findings limit where either encoder can be trusted.
2 Introduction
Reading an EEG automatically (deciding whether a recording looks "normal" or "abnormal", or telling a stroke patient's brain activity apart from a healthy control's) runs into the same wall most clinical machine learning does: labelled EEG is expensive. It takes a trained neurologist to read and annotate every recording, so the datasets available for supervised learning stay small next to what made deep learning work for images or text. The standard fix is self-supervised pre-training. Train a big encoder on a task that needs no labels, then re-use it on the small labelled task you actually care about.
Meta AI introduced I-JEPA in 2023 [2] and V-JEPA in 2024 [3]. Instead of reconstructing missing pixels, the model predicts the embedding of a missing region from the embedding of the visible region, using a slowly-updated (“momentum”) copy of itself as the target. The masking drives the learning. Mask enough regions, and the only way the context encoder keeps succeeding at this prediction is to learn how the signal's parts fit together. Raw pixel values (or raw voltage samples, for EEG) are full of detail an encoder would waste capacity reproducing exactly. Predicting in embedding space lets it skip that and keep what is actually predictable. That tends to be what a downstream classifier needs anyway. It also sidesteps a known failure mode of predictive self-supervision: representation collapse, where the easiest way to shrink the loss is to output the same vector for everything. Keeping the two networks from sharing weights directly is what stops that from happening.

Figure 1: The JEPA idea: predict the target encoder's embedding of a masked/future region from the context encoder's embedding, never the raw pixels [2, 3].
EEG-VJEPA (Hojjati et al., 2025, arXiv:2507.03633) [1] carries this recipe over to EEG by treating a multi-channel recording as a short silent video: channels stand in for spatial rows, time windows for video frames. The authors report their model, pre-trained this way and evaluated on TUAB, reaches an AUROC around 0.877–0.879, ahead of prior published baselines. They also released their pre-trained checkpoints and code, so picking this paper felt like the safe option: reproduce a recent, credible-looking result, understand why it works, then push it further on a harder or different task. The “safe” part turned out to be the wrong assumption.
We first tried, as faithfully as we could, to reproduce the paper's own TUAB result with its own released weights and its own evaluation code. That did not succeed, and the reason turned out to be diagnosable in its own right: the released checkpoints are collapsed. We used that as the starting point for the rest of the project (“Act 0”, in our own naming, since everything else the course builds on sits downstream of it): we designed our own pre-trained encoder, FEI+GTJ, validated it on the dataset it was trained on (NMT), tested it on TUAB, a different hospital's data, both zero-shot (pre-trained on NMT only) and after adding TUAB's unlabelled recordings to pre-training, and finally applied it to a third, out-of-domain task (separating stroke patients from healthy controls) where a well-established clinical baseline (the Brain Symmetry Index) let us check whether our features add anything over classical signal processing.
3 Design
3.1 Problem Formulation (PO(b))
3.1.1 Identification of Scope
Our original course proposal, submitted in week 5, had two parts. Part 1: reproduce EEG-VJEPA's published TUAB result using the authors' released checkpoints, and explain any gap. Part 2: apply the same frozen encoders, together with a classical clinical feature (the Brain Symmetry Index), to a healthy-vs-stroke task on an independent cohort. A model that only works on the one benchmark it was tuned for is not worth much.
A third item was in the original proposal too: pre-training our own copy of EEG-VJEPA from scratch. We dropped it partway through the term. By then it was clear that working out why the released model collapses, and building a replacement that doesn't, would use our remaining weeks and our one GPU better than a from-scratch run would.
We call this scope “Act 0” internally: reproduction, diagnosis, first working replacement. It does not cover the motor-imagery work the team has since started on a different task; that is out of scope here and shows up only in Future Work.
3.1.2 Literature Review
The JEPA family anchors one corner of this: I-JEPA (Assran et al., 2023) [2] and its video extension V-JEPA [3] predict embeddings instead of pixels, building on BYOL's momentum-encoder idea (Grill et al., 2020) [4]: a slowly-updated target network, no negative pairs, and in principle no collapse. Experiment 3 below shows that safeguard can still be switched off elsewhere in a training config.
A much larger body of work is EEG foundation models proper. Besides EEG-VJEPA, two bigger EEG transformers came out around the same time: CBraMod (Wang et al., 2025) [5] and LaBraM (Jiang et al., 2024) [6], both pre-trained on tens of thousands of hours of pooled EEG. CBraMod reports about 0.83 balanced accuracy / 0.92 AUROC on TUAB; LaBraM, about 0.81 / 0.90. These set the current bar, and gave us a sense of how a large, well-resourced EEG model is normally reported.
NMT (Khan et al., 2022) [7] is the open, South-Asian-population source we pre-train on and cross-validate against. TUAB (López et al., 2015) [8] is the U.S. benchmark the EEG-VJEPA paper targets. The Brain Symmetry Index (van Putten & Tavy, 2004; van Putten, 2007 [9, 10]) is a decades-old hand-crafted feature that tracks left-right power asymmetry and is known to track stroke severity. It's a real yardstick for the stroke experiments; we didn't pick it because it's weak. One more reference we almost missed: a methodological preprint on stress-testing EEG foundation models with negative controls (arXiv:2607.24519 [11]) surfaced partway through the project, well after we had already started comparing every pre-trained model against a random-init twin on the same pipeline. It turned out to be arguing for the exact habit we'd already fallen into: welcome confirmation, but not something that changed our approach.
3.1.3 Formulation of Problem
Concretely, the project chases one question after another, each answer setting up the next. Do the released EEG-VJEPA checkpoints reproduce their own paper's TUAB result, under a faithful re-implementation of the authors' own evaluation protocol, and if not, why not? If they don't, can we build a self-supervised EEG encoder, trained entirely within our own compute budget, that actually beats a hand-crafted classical baseline on the data it was trained on, and actually transfers that benefit, not just its architecture, to a recording from a different hospital? And if that holds up, does the same encoder, frozen and unchanged, add anything on a third, structurally different task (stroke vs. healthy), measured directly against an established clinical feature?
3.1.4 Analysis
Each question above maps to a specific check, spelled out in full in §5.3.1: question (1) against a random, untrained control (exactly what caught the collapse in Experiment 1); question (2) with paired statistics rather than a bare point estimate, since our folds, cohorts, and seeds are all small enough for a raw accuracy number to just be noise; question (3) against the Brain Symmetry Index, a bar already established by classical neuroscience rather than one we picked ourselves.
3.2 Design Method (PO(a))
The released EEG-VJEPA [1] treats a 19-channel, 5-second EEG window as a single-frame “video clip”: channels take the place of one spatial axis, short time-slices take the place of the other, and a Vision Transformer (ViT-M or ViT-B) patch-embeds this grid exactly as it would a video frame. During pre-training, V-JEPA masks out spatiotemporal blocks of this grid and trains a context encoder to predict a momentum target encoder's embedding of the masked blocks.

Figure 2: How the released EEG-VJEPA reframes a multi-channel EEG window as a single video frame for its Vision Transformer encoder [1].
Concretely: each 19×500 frame is cut into patches of 4 channels × 30 samples, and groups of 4 consecutive frames are joined into one “tubelet” (the same trick ordinary V-JEPA [3] uses to turn a short video clip into a manageable number of tokens). This yields 512 tokens per clip, each described by 384 numbers for ViT-M, which then pass through 12 transformer blocks: self-attention with 6 heads per block, so every token can draw on every other token, followed by a small 384→1,536→384 MLP inside each block (ViT-B instead uses 768-number tokens, 12 heads, and 16-frame clips).

Figure 3: EEG-VJEPA's Vision Transformer — patch and tubelet sizes, the resulting 512-token × 384-number grid, and the stack of self-attention blocks that process it [1].
The three-part JEPA structure that this report refers back to throughout is, in the released model's form: a context encoder that only ever sees the visible tokens of a clip; a target encoder — an exponential-moving-average (EMA), never directly trained copy of the context encoder — that sees the entire, unmasked clip, whose outputs at the masked positions become the prediction targets; and a small predictor network that takes the context encoder's tokens plus learned “mask tokens” marking the hidden positions, and tries to predict the target encoder's output there. Only the embedding is predicted, never the raw signal. The released checkpoint's training loss is the mean L1 distance between predicted and target embeddings, a choice we return to directly in Experiment 3. Both of our own encoders below keep the same three parts (online encoder, EMA target encoder, predictor) but change what each encoder sees: in FEI the online encoder sees the full window and the target encoder a frequency-masked copy of it; in GTJ the online encoder sees a window's past and the target encoder its future.
Concretely, the target encoder's weights are never touched by gradient descent at all: after every training step they are nudged a small amount towards the context (“online”) encoder's current weights [1], θ_target ← a·θ_target + (1 − a)·θ_online, with momentum a = 0.996 throughout this report (so each update moves the target only 0.4% of the way towards the online encoder). This keeps the prediction target almost static from one step to the next, which is what lets the predictor chase a slowly-moving goal instead of one that shifts as fast as the network producing it.

Figure 4: Spatiotemporal block masking — the context encoder only ever sees the unshaded tokens; the target encoder sees every token and supplies the prediction targets at the shaded, masked positions [1].
Once we had established (Experiments 1–4, Results section) that this released checkpoint is collapsed and cannot be rescued by fine-tuning, we designed our own encoder pair from scratch, built around two principles the released model's failure pointed us towards: mask in the frequency domain, where pathological EEG slowing shows up directly as a spectral shift that a time-domain patch mask can miss; and keep the architecture small and matched to the dataset size (NMT has only a few thousand recordings, far fewer than the millions of video clips typical JEPA training uses), so that the encoder is not so over-parameterised that the easiest solution to its pretext task is to ignore the input.
FEI is a 1-D convolutional encoder. Its pretext task removes a random fraction — anywhere from 0% to 70% of rFFT frequency bins, redrawn independently for every window (`U(0, 0.7)` in code/fei_pretrain.py) — of the signal's frequency content (via an FFT mask) and asks an online encoder to predict, in embedding space, what a slowly-updated momentum copy of itself sees when given the masked window, plus a small auxiliary term that predicts which bands were removed. Both prediction targets are L2-normalised before the loss is taken (a BYOL-style trick, equivalent to a bounded 2–2·cos(pred, target) loss), which is the single biggest lever against collapse: an un-normalised MSE loss lets the network cheat by shrinking its output scale to zero.
class FEI(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_freq, d=256, h=128, in_ch=NCH):
        super().__init__()
        self.enc, self.proj = Encoder(d, ch=in_ch), nn.Linear(d, h)        # online
        self.enc_t, self.proj_t = Encoder(d, ch=in_ch), nn.Linear(d, h)    # momentum target
        self._sync(); [p.requires_grad_(False) for p in self._tgt_params()]
        self.mask_enc = nn.Linear(n_freq, h, bias=False)   # mask prompt -> embedding space
        self.z1, self.z2 = mlp(h), mlp(h)                  # predictors
 
    def forward(self, x, xp, M):
        e = self.enc(x); u = self.proj(e)                  # online, original window
        with torch.no_grad():
            up = self.proj_t(self.enc_t(xp))               # momentum, masked window
        m = self.mask_enc(M)
        uhat = self.z1(u + m.detach())                     # predict masked embedding
        mhat = self.z2(u.detach() - up)                     # predict which bands were removed
        nrm = lambda a: F.normalize(a, dim=-1)              # BYOL-style: bounded loss, no scale collapse
        l_embed = F.mse_loss(nrm(uhat), nrm(up), reduction="none").mean(-1)
        return l_embed.mean() + F.mse_loss(nrm(mhat), nrm(m)), e, l_embed.detach()
 
def freq_mask(x, bias=0.0, b1=0.0, b2=0.7):
    """Zero a random U(b1,b2) fraction of rFFT bins (shared across channels)."""
    Xf = torch.fft.rfft(x, dim=-1)                          # (B,19,n) complex
    ratio = torch.empty(x.shape[0], 1, device=x.device).uniform_(b1, b2)
    M = (torch.rand(x.shape[0], Xf.shape[-1], device=x.device) < ratio).float()
    xp = torch.fft.irfft(Xf * (1 - M).unsqueeze(1), n=x.shape[-1], dim=-1)
    return xp.float(), M
Code Snippet 1: FEI's momentum-encoder JEPA loss and its frequency-domain masking function (code/fei_pretrain.py, simplified — the full file also implements an adaptive, difficulty-weighted masking variant, `--mask_bias`, whose only use in this report is in the fused scores of GTJ's window-length sweep later in this section).
Written out once, for one window x (19 channels × L samples, L = 1,000 in pre-training): freq_mask draws a removal rate r ~ U(0, 0.7), removes each of the 501 rFFT bins independently with probability r (the same bins on every channel, recorded in a binary mask M), and inverts the FFT to give the masked copy x′ = irfft(rfft(x)·(1 − M)). The online path embeds the original window, u = proj(enc(x)); the target path embeds the masked copy without gradient, u′ = proj_t(enc_t(x′)); and the mask itself is embedded as a prompt, m = mask_enc(M). The two predictors are û = z1(u + sg(m)) and m̂ = z2(sg(u) − u′), where sg(·) is stop-gradient; with n(v) = v/‖v‖ and h = 128, the loss, averaged over the batch, is
ℓ = (1/h)·‖n(û) − n(u′)‖² + (1/h)·‖n(m̂) − n(m)‖²
The first term trains the encoder, its projection and z1; the second trains only z2 and the mask-prompt encoder, because sg(·) stops its gradient from reaching u. The target weights (enc_t, proj_t) are never trained by gradient: after each step they move towards the online weights by the EMA rule above, with a = 0.996.

Figure 5: One FEI pre-training step for a batch of 4 windows — frequency masking produces the masked copy x′ and the mask M; the online encoder embeds the original window x while the EMA target encoder embeds the masked copy; prediction 1 recovers the target's masked-window embedding from the online embedding plus the mask prompt, prediction 2 recovers the mask prompt from the difference between the two embeddings; the summed loss updates the online branch and the mask-prompt encoder by AdamW and the target branch by EMA.

Figure 6: freq_mask applied to one real NMT channel's 5-second window — the original spectrum with the bins a real draw removed marked in red (a), the realized binary mask itself, 208 of 501 bins (42%) removed in this draw (b), a zoom on the alpha band (6-14 Hz) showing exactly which bins survive (c), and the resulting time-domain signal before and after masking and inverse-FFT (d).
Both loss terms in Code Snippet 1 compare normalized embeddings, which quietly turns a mean-squared-error loss into a cosine-similarity loss. For unit vectors â and b̂, ‖â − b̂‖² = ‖â‖² + ‖b̂‖² − 2·(â·b̂) = 2 − 2·cos(â, b̂), so minimizing F.mse_loss(nrm(uhat), nrm(up)) is mathematically equivalent to maximizing the cosine similarity between the predicted and target embeddings. This is also why the explicit `F.normalize` call matters: without it, the network could trivially drive the loss towards zero by shrinking its output scale instead of actually learning to predict the masked content's direction in embedding space.
Structurally, the `Encoder` inside FEI is a small 1-D convolutional network rather than a transformer: four blocks, each one Conv1d (kernel 7, stride 2) → BatchNorm → GELU, with the channel count growing 19→64→128→128→256 while stride 2 halves the time length at every block; an average over the remaining time steps then gives 256 numbers, and a final Linear layer produces the 256-number embedding used everywhere else in this report.

FEI is trained with AdamW (learning rate 2 × 10⁻⁴, weight decay 10⁻⁴), batch size 128, for 60 epochs, drawing 4 random 5-second windows per recording per epoch (`--wpe` in code/fei_pretrain.py) rather than always the same crop. At probe time, a recording's single 256-number embedding is its online encoder's output mean-pooled over every non-overlapping window in that recording (`embed_recording`), not one arbitrarily chosen window. Because the encoder ends in a global average over time it accepts any window length, and every FEI number in Experiments 5, 6 and 8 embeds 20-second (4,000-sample) windows, the same length GTJ uses, even though FEI is pre-trained on 5-second ones.
GTJ (GRU-based Time-JEPA) targets a different weakness: a hand-crafted band-power feature averages spectral power over an entire window, throwing away how that spectrum evolves moment to moment. GTJ instead keeps a short-time Fourier transform (STFT) spectrogram (frequency × time) and trains a GRU-based encoder to predict the embedding of the future portion of a recording's spectrogram from the embedding of its past portion. Because the future frames are structurally never shown to the context encoder, this pretext task cannot be trivially shortcut the way an earlier masked-input design we tried could (see Novelty, below).

Figure 8: GTJ turns each EEG window into a log-power spectrogram (frequency × time) and trains a GRU encoder to predict the future span's embedding from the past span's embedding.
def spectrogram(x):
    """(B,19,L) -> (B, T, 19*NF) log-power frames: a temporal SEQUENCE of spectra."""
    z = torch.stft(x.reshape(B_*C, L), n_fft=NFFT, hop_length=HOP, win_length=NFFT,
                   window=_WIN.to(x.device), center=False, return_complex=True)
    p = (z.real ** 2 + z.imag ** 2 + 1e-6).log()                 # log-power
    return p.reshape(B_, C, NF, -1).permute(0, 3, 1, 2).reshape(B_, -1, C * NF)
 
class DynEncoder(nn.Module):
    """Per-frame spectral embed + GRU over time -> summary of a frame span."""
    def __init__(self, d=256, in_dim=None):
        super().__init__()
        self.bn = nn.BatchNorm1d(in_dim); self.embed = nn.Linear(in_dim, d)
        self.gru = nn.GRU(d, d, batch_first=True)
 
    def forward(self, z):                                        # z: (B,T,in_dim)
        h = torch.relu(self.embed(self.bn(z.reshape(-1, z.shape[-1])).reshape(z.shape)))
        out, _ = self.gru(h)
        return out[:, -1]                                        # last hidden = summary of the span
 
class DynJEPA(nn.Module):
    """Predict FUTURE-span embedding from PAST-span embedding. BYOL-style, EMA target."""
    def forward(self, z):
        past, future = z[:, :TC], z[:, TC:]
        u = self.proj(self.enc(past))
        with torch.no_grad():
            v = self.proj_t(self.enc_t(future))
        nrm = lambda a: F.normalize(a, dim=-1)
        return F.mse_loss(nrm(self.pred(u)), nrm(v))

"EMA target" means the target encoder (enc_t, proj_t) is never trained directly: it is an exponential-moving-average copy of the main encoder's weights, updated slowly after every step, which keeps the prediction target stable instead of chasing a network that is changing as fast as the one being trained.
The same idea plays out here as a picture, for one concrete batch: the spectrogram splits into past and future spans, each summarized by the shared DynEncoder, and the past span's predictor output is pushed towards the EMA target's future-span embedding.

GTJ shares FEI's optimizer and schedule exactly (AdamW, learning rate 2 × 10⁻⁴, weight decay 10⁻⁴, batch size 128, 60 epochs, 4 random windows per recording per epoch), and a recording's embedding at probe time is likewise its online encoder's output mean-pooled over every non-overlapping 20-second window (`embed_dyn` in code/fei_branchC.py). Table 2's particular window length and recurrent unit were themselves the product of a dose-response check, not an arbitrary choice. Holding everything else fixed and sweeping the window from 5 s to 20 s to 40 s (`branchC_cv_results{,_h20,_h40}.json`), GTJ alone scored 0.820, 0.836 and 0.823 AUROC, and the gap between its learned embedding and a random-init encoder of identical architecture widened monotonically — +0.021 (4/5 folds), +0.038 (5/5), +0.045 (5/5) — the signature of a model actually exploiting temporal dynamics, rather than of an architecture whose untrained transform alone does the work. Swapping the GRU for a long-memory Linear Recurrent Unit (LRU) [12] (`LRUEncoder` in code/fei_branchC.py, a diagonal recurrence h_t = λ⊙h_{t−1} + γ⊙(Bx_t) with λ initialised near 1 for long memory) at 40 s left GTJ essentially unchanged (0.819 ± 0.010 vs. the GRU's 0.823 ± 0.009, from `branchC_cv_results_h40lru.json`). So the 40 s dip reflects a longer, more stationary window carrying less to predict, not the GRU running out of memory — which is why the cheaper GRU at 20 s is this report's GTJ configuration throughout. The same sweep's fused scores (0.858, 0.870 and 0.863, and 0.861 with the LRU) pair GTJ with the adaptive-masking FEI variant noted under Code Snippet 1 rather than the plain FEI used everywhere else in this report, which is why its 20-second figure (0.870 ± 0.023) differs from Table 11's 0.865. One caveat: the 20-second window was chosen on these same seed-0 folds, with no nested inner split, so GTJ's 20-second numbers carry some selection optimism. They do not depend on it, though: GTJ beats Table 11's band power (0.786) at every window length in the sweep (0.820, 0.836 and 0.823).
Both branches are frozen after pre-training and their 256-dimensional embeddings are concatenated (FEI+GTJ, 512-dimensional) before a standard StandardScaler + class-balanced logistic-regression probe is trained on top (the same lightweight, low-capacity classifier used throughout this report for every model), so that comparisons between encoders are never confounded by one model getting a more powerful classifier than another.

Two preprocessing families run through this report, and Table 4 lists exactly which experiment uses which. The released EEG-VJEPA checkpoints only accept the input they were pre-trained on, so every recording they see is prepared with the paper's own pipeline (§4.1 of arXiv:2507.03633) [1], followed as closely as its text allows (the three points it leaves open are listed after Code Snippet 3): pick and reorder the same 19 standard 10–20 channels, crop to a fixed 5-minute window, band-pass filter to 1–40 Hz, resample to 100 Hz, z-score each channel, and cut the signal into 5-second frames with 50% overlap (Code Snippet 3). The TUAB band-power baseline of Experiments 4 and 6 reads the same 100 Hz frames. FEI and GTJ instead work at 200 Hz, NMT's native rate: on NMT they read recordings that are only per-channel z-scored (no band-pass, no resampling) and stored as 2.5-second frames; on TUAB and the stroke cohort each recording is resampled to 200 Hz and z-scored, with a 0.5–40 Hz band-pass on TUAB, and a common average reference plus the spectral-matching step described below on stroke. We deliberately re-derived the 100 Hz pipeline from the paper's description rather than assuming our own earlier 200 Hz preprocessing would be compatible with the released checkpoints. An early, since-resolved concern in this project was whether a sample-rate mismatch could explain any gap to the paper; it could not, but getting the released checkpoints' expected input exactly right was an essential and non-obvious first step.
The z-score step is a per-channel, per-recording standardization: for each channel's raw samples x, z = (x − μ) / σ, where μ and σ are that channel's own mean and standard deviation over the cropped recording (substituting σ = 1 on the rare flat channel where σ = 0, to avoid dividing by zero). Standardizing per recording rather than with a fixed, dataset-wide μ/σ matters here because electrode impedance and amplifier gain vary enough between recordings, and between the NMT and TUAB hospitals in particular, that a single global scale would leave some recordings under- or over-saturated relative to what the encoder saw during pre-training.
# Paper preprocessing (arXiv:2507.03633 §4.1), as closely as its text allows:
CHANNELS = ['FP1', 'FP2', 'F3', 'F4', 'C3', 'C4', 'P3', 'P4', 'O1', 'O2',
            'F7', 'F8', 'T3', 'T4', 'T5', 'T6', 'FZ', 'CZ', 'PZ']   # this order
TARGET_FS, WINDOW_SIZE = 100, 500   # 100 Hz, 5 s frames
CROP_SECONDS = 300      # crop to a fixed 5-minute length
BANDPASS = (1.0, 40.0)  # bandpass 1-40 Hz (encoder was pretrained on this band)
WINDOW_STRIDE = 250     # 50% overlap -> (30000-500)//250 + 1 = 119 frames (paper: 118)
 
def preprocess_edf(edf_path, out_path, crop_start=0.0):
    raw = mne.io.read_raw_edf(edf_path, preload=False, verbose=False)
    raw.pick_channels(CHANNELS); raw.reorder_channels(CHANNELS)
    t0 = min(crop_start, max(0.0, raw.times[-1] - CROP_SECONDS))
    raw.crop(tmin=t0, tmax=min(t0 + CROP_SECONDS, raw.times[-1]))
    raw.load_data(verbose=False)
    raw.filter(BANDPASS[0], BANDPASS[1], verbose=False)
    if round(raw.info['sfreq']) != TARGET_FS:
        raw.resample(TARGET_FS)
    data = raw.get_data()
    mean, std = data.mean(1, keepdims=True), data.std(1, keepdims=True)
    data = (data - mean) / np.where(std == 0, 1.0, std)         # per-channel z-score
    num_frames = (data.shape[1] - WINDOW_SIZE) // WINDOW_STRIDE + 1
    frames = np.stack([data[:, i*WINDOW_STRIDE : i*WINDOW_STRIDE+WINDOW_SIZE]
                        for i in range(num_frames)]).astype(np.float32)
    np.save(out_path, frames)                                    # atomic (tmp + os.replace) in full code
Code Snippet 3: The paper's own §4.1 preprocessing [1], which prepares every recording the released checkpoints see (code/preprocess_nmt.py, simplified; the stroke cohort gets the same filtering, resampling, z-scoring and framing in code/stroke_phaseB.py, after a common average reference).
Three points are left open by §4.1, and Code Snippet 3 settles each one. It filters at the native sampling rate and then resamples, where the paper lists downsampling before filtering; it z-scores each channel over the whole cropped recording, where the paper's “last step” could also be read as per window (both readings give the same collapsed output from the released ViT-M, Experiment 3); and it keeps every full 500-sample frame at a 250-sample stride, which gives (30,000 − 500) / 250 + 1 = 119 frames per 5-minute crop rather than the 118 the paper states. All 2,993 preprocessed TUAB files have shape 119 × 19 × 500. Filtering and resampling use MNE 1.8.0's defaults (a zero-phase FIR band-pass, then FFT-based resampling). A recording shorter than 5 minutes is kept at its full length, and one missing any of the 19 channels fails channel selection and is skipped (the script logs it and moves on). The released encoder reads clips of 32 frames spread evenly over 96 consecutive frames, so a 119-frame recording offers 23 clip positions. It also cuts each 19 × 500 frame into patches of 4 channels × 30 samples, so only the first 16 channel rows and the first 480 samples are ever embedded: in the paper's channel order the three midline channels, FZ, CZ and PZ, come last and never reach the released encoder.

Figure 11: Cropping a continuous EEG recording into fixed-length, 50%-overlapping windows for the encoder input.
The stroke cohort also needed two preprocessing steps the other two datasets do not. First, a purely data-quality step: any channel the dataset's own authors had flagged as bad is rebuilt from its neighbours by spherical-spline interpolation before anything else is done to the recording. Second, a non-obvious step described in full in §5.3.3 (Experiment 8): because its recording hardware and montage differ from NMT's, its raw spectrum does not match what our NMT-trained encoders expect to see, and GTJ in particular is sensitive to this mismatch. We built a label-free, per-channel spectral-matching filter that maps the stroke cohort's mean spectrum onto NMT's before any encoder sees it, fixing this without ever looking at the stroke/control labels. The filter is fitted once on all 15 subjects pooled, so it is transductive (each held-out subject helps shape the filter applied to it), and it was added after the pre-registered analysis had been run; Appendix §11.2 gives the record and a fold-local check. Here and throughout, “pre-registered” means a locally timestamped pre-analysis protocol (Appendix §11.2), not an entry in a public registry.

One formula named in Table 5 but never actually shown: the classical band-power baseline behind every “band power” row in Experiments 4 and 6. For each channel, Welch's method estimates a power spectral density from the already-filtered, already-windowed signal; summing that density inside five fixed bands and dividing by their total gives each band's relative power — a channel's spectral shape with its absolute amplitude divided out, which keeps the comparison fair on top of input that is already per-recording z-scored (§3.3). Experiment 5's NMT band-power arm applies the same construction to NMT's own 200 Hz input (code/baseline_bandpower.py): Welch over the whole recording rather than per frame, with bands 0.5–4, 4–8, 8–13, 13–30 and 30–45 Hz, normalised by total 0.5–45 Hz power.
BANDS = [(1, 4), (4, 8), (8, 13), (13, 30), (30, 40)]  # delta/theta/alpha/beta/gamma; data already 1-40 Hz band-passed
FS = 100                                                # Hz, matching the shared preprocessing pipeline (Code Snippet 3)
 
def rbp(path):
    x = np.load(path)                                   # (119, 19, 500): 5 s frames, 50% overlap
    f, p = welch(x, fs=FS, nperseg=250, axis=-1)        # Welch PSD per frame
    p = p.mean(0)                                        # average over the 119 frames -> (19, freqs)
    bp = np.stack([p[:, (f >= lo) & (f < hi)].sum(-1) for lo, hi in BANDS], -1)
    return (bp / bp.sum(-1, keepdims=True)).ravel()      # (95,) relative band power = 19 channels x 5 bands

def fit_probe(Xtr, ytr, Xte, yte):
    """The ONE probe used for every encoder in this report: StandardScaler + balanced LogReg, C=1."""
    sc = StandardScaler().fit(Xtr)
    clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000, class_weight="balanced", C=1.0).fit(sc.transform(Xtr), ytr)
    s = clf.decision_function(sc.transform(Xte))
    return roc_auc_score(yte, s), balanced_accuracy_score(yte, (s > 0).astype(int))

4 Implementation
4.1 Description
Nothing was combined before it was tested on its own. In the weeks before the mid-term break (around week 7), FEI was built and unit-tested in isolation: its self-test checks that masking a signal's 10 Hz component actually nulls a 10 Hz test tone, and that the JEPA loss drops over a few optimisation steps on a synthetic batch, before any real EEG reaches it. GTJ got the same treatment in week 8. Its self-test checks spectrogram shapes, checks the loss drops, and checks that the past and future halves of a spectrogram aren't numerically identical; otherwise “predict the future” is trivially solvable and means nothing.
One data-loading problem showed up before either encoder was even running: an early version of the NMT loader pulled every recording fully into memory and hit a 24 GB out-of-memory error. Switching to a memory-mapped loader fixed that (down to about 2 GB), and, as a side effect, training ran roughly ten times faster, since the GPU was no longer waiting on disk reads. We should have done that from day one.
Only once FEI and GTJ had each passed their own self-tests and their own single-branch NMT cross-validation (Experiment 5) were they fused into FEI+GTJ. The same build-alone-then-integrate pattern repeated for every later addition. The band-power and BSI baselines were checked against known clinical effects before being used as a comparison bar (the pipeline-soundness gate in Experiment 7).
Every long pre-training run checkpoints its state to disk after each cross-validation fold. The shared GPU has, more than once, needed to be freed for another job, or paused outright by the thermal guard; a resumed run just picks up from the last completed fold instead of starting over.

5 Design Analysis and Evaluation
5.1 Novelty
Neither FEI nor GTJ is architecturally new by itself. Removing random frequency components of a time series is already a pre-training augmentation in time-frequency-consistency contrastive learning [14]; FEI's twist is to predict, in embedding space, how a momentum target encoder sees the masked copy. Predicting a signal's future from its past is what contrastive and autoregressive predictive coding already do for speech [15, 16], and contrastive predictive coding has already been applied to clinical EEG, including pathology detection [17]. Two recent EEG papers come closer still: one pre-trains by autoregressively predicting future spectro-temporal content [18], and a 2026 preprint that appeared during this project predicts future latent EEG states with an EMA target encoder [19].
We found that a specific, publicly released EEG foundation-model checkpoint is representation-collapsed. We could not find this diagnosis published at the time. Token standard deviation and cross-recording cosine similarity make it simple enough to re-run on that checkpoint in minutes.
FEI+GTJ is our working two-branch replacement. An earlier GTJ design masked out spectrogram regions and asked the encoder to reconstruct them, and Ahmed found it could be solved almost entirely from each window's average spectral content: the network wasn't learning much more than the band-power baseline already in this report. Moving to a past-predicts-future design, where the future half is never shown to the context encoder at all, closed that shortcut. The resulting pair is validated end-to-end, with paired statistics, across three datasets and two clinically distinct tasks.
GTJ later collapsed on a different dataset because its BatchNorm layers carried NMT's input statistics across domains. We diagnosed that failure and corrected it without labels. The fix may generalise to other EEG self-supervised encoders that normalise their input the same way, but we have tested it only on the cohort it was designed on.
5.2 Design Considerations (PO(c))
5.2.1 Considerations to public health and safety
A model that flags EEG recordings as “abnormal” touches patient safety directly. A false negative could delay a diagnosis. A false positive could trigger unnecessary follow-up tests, or just worry someone unnecessarily. Our failure-axis analysis (§5.3.4) found error rates are not uniform across age groups: FEI over-flags healthy young children as abnormal at more than six times the rate it does healthy adults. A system with that gap has no business sitting near a real clinical decision for a paediatric patient, whatever its average AUROC says.
5.2.2 Considerations to environment
Every model here is intentionally small (1.15M parameters for FEI and 1.88M for GTJ, counting both the online and the EMA target copy; Tables 1–2) and trained for tens, not hundreds, of epochs on a single consumer GPU, rather than the institutional clusters that CBraMod and LaBraM pre-train on. Partly this was just what hardware we had. But it's also a real result: a model sized to match its dataset, instead of scaled up regardless of how much data is available, already beats a classical baseline and, with TUAB's unlabelled recordings added to pre-training, scores close to a released large-scale checkpoint under the same linear probe on TUAB (0.862 vs 0.838, not significantly different). Every run is also gated by a thermal guard (§5.4) that pauses or aborts a job if CPU temperature crosses a safe threshold, which caps energy draw as a side effect.
5.2.3 Considerations to cultural and societal needs
NMT, which underlies most of this project, was collected in Pakistan specifically to give under-represented, lower-resource clinical populations a presence in EEG machine-learning research. Most large datasets, TUAB included, come from well-resourced North American and European hospitals. Showing that a small encoder built on this dataset transfers its pre-training benefit to a different hospital's data (§5.3.3, Experiment 6) is one small piece of evidence that self-supervised EEG models don't have to depend on high-resource-setting data alone. Stroke, the third task here, is one of the leading causes of long-term disability worldwide; low-cost, automatable screening matters most exactly where specialist neurological review is hardest to get.
5.3 Investigations (PO(d))
5.3.1 Design of Experiment
Every experiment here follows the same rules. Any claim that pre-training “helped” needs a paired comparison against the same architecture with random, untrained weights, run through identical evaluation code. Every reported difference between two models comes with a confidence interval or a p-value: bootstrap resampling for independent recordings, paired tests for same-subset comparisons (for cross-validation folds, whose training sets overlap, the Nadeau–Bengio corrected resampled t-test [20], with Holm's adjustment [21] across a family of comparisons), label-permutation tests for small cohorts, whichever fits the data at hand. And experiments are ordered by increasing domain shift: validate on the data an encoder was trained on first (NMT), then transfer it, frozen, to a different hospital's data (TUAB), and only then apply it to a structurally different clinical task it was never designed for (stroke vs. healthy). The TUAB step is run zero-shot for all three encoders, pre-trained on NMT only, and again for GTJ and the fused model after adding TUAB's unlabelled training recordings to pre-training (Experiment 6). Working at the first, easiest step is a much weaker claim than surviving all three.
Two recurring checks in this design are, in effect, this report's ablation study: the random-init control run alongside almost every pre-trained encoder (removing “pre-training” as the only variable, with architecture and evaluation code held fixed) and Experiment 4's comparison across feature/probe choices (removing or swapping the encoder itself, with the classifier held fixed). Rather than a single dedicated ablation table, this project's ablations are distributed across the experiments below at the point where each one is most informative.
5.3.2 Data Collection
Dataset acquisition and preparation are described in §3.3 above; Table 3 there has size, role, and any licensing caveats for all three datasets.
5.3.3 Results and Analysis
We use AUROC everywhere. It doesn't care about the decision threshold, which matters because we never settled on one. Balanced accuracy and AUC-PR show up alongside it where useful. Unless stated otherwise, “±” is the standard deviation across cross-validation folds or training subsets, and bracketed ranges are 95% bootstrap confidence intervals.
Formally, writing TP/FP/TN/FN for true/false positives/negatives at a given threshold, sensitivity (true-positive rate) is TPR = TP / (TP + FN) and specificity's complement (false-positive rate) is FPR = FP / (FP + TN); AUROC is the area under the curve traced by (FPR, TPR) as the decision threshold sweeps over every possible value, equivalently the probability that a randomly chosen abnormal recording is scored higher than a randomly chosen normal one. Balanced accuracy fixes a single threshold (predicted probability p > 0.5 throughout this report, equivalently decision_function s > 0 for the logistic-regression probe of Code Snippet 5, which is scikit-learn 1.5.2's LogisticRegression with C = 1, balanced class weights and otherwise default settings: L2 penalty, lbfgs solver, tolerance 10⁻⁴) and averages the two classes' recall so neither is drowned out by the other's size: BAcc = ½·(TPR + TNR), where TNR = TN / (TN + FP). AUC-PR is the same sweep-every-threshold idea as AUROC but traces precision = TP / (TP + FP) against recall = TPR instead of FPR against TPR, which makes it more sensitive to performance on the minority (here, abnormal or stroke) class than AUROC is.
Experiment 1 — Frozen reproduction of the paper's TUAB result.
The most basic question came first: load the authors' own released checkpoints (SHA-256-verified against the Hugging Face file records; hashes in Table 18) into the authors' own evaluation heads, run them on the authors' own official 276-recording TUAB eval set, and see whether we land anywhere near their reported 0.877 (ViT-M) / 0.879 (ViT-B) AUROC. To rule out our own code before blaming theirs, Maruf diffed our pipeline against the authors' public repository (github.com/amir-hojjati/eeg-vjepa) at commit c739fad5ad57ec4439a094343901a245edf611ec: 43 of 48 compared files were byte-identical. The other 5 (app/main.py, app/vjepa/train.py, eval.py, schedulers.py and attentive_pooler.py) carry clearly-labelled additions of ours — a thermal guard, a fixed evaluation seed, an optional alternate head, an optional learning-rate scale, balanced accuracy among the metrics — that don't change the computation at their defaults.
The same evaluation heads recur throughout the experiments below — a Linear Probe, the paper's own §4.2 Attention Classifier, and its Fig-3 Attentive Probe [1] — each sitting on top of a frozen (or, in two cases, fine-tuned) encoder and turning its output into a normal/abnormal decision.

Figure 13: The Linear Probe — mean and standard deviation pooled over all tokens (or, for FEI/GTJ, the raw embedding), then a StandardScaler + logistic regression.
The Linear Probe is the simplest of the three: for ViT-M, it takes the mean and standard deviation of the 512 output tokens (384 + 384 = 768 numbers), averaged over every clip position in a recording; for FEI/GTJ, it is simply their 256(+256)-number embedding directly. Either way, a single `StandardScaler` + class-balanced `LogisticRegression` sits on top (the same lightweight head used for every encoder in this report), so that no comparison between models is confounded by one getting a more powerful classifier than another.

Figure 14: The paper's §4.2 Attention Classifier — a learned score per token, softmax-normalised into weights, a weighted average, then a linear classifier [1].
The §4.2 Attention Classifier (`AttentionClassifier` in the authors' own code) is the head the paper itself evaluates with, and the only one of the three ever used for fine-tuning. It has just two learned layers: a Linear(384→1) scores every one of the 512 tokens, softmax turns those 512 scores into weights that sum to 1, the tokens are averaged with these weights into a single 384-number vector, so the head effectively learns which tokens to listen to, and a final Linear(384→2) reads off the normal/abnormal decision. We use it both frozen (on the released checkpoint) and as the head trained end-to-end during fine-tuning and from-scratch training (Experiment 2) [1].

Figure 15: The paper's Fig-3 Attentive Probe — a learnable query token attends over all 512 tokens via full multi-head cross-attention, followed by an MLP [1].
The Fig-3 Attentive Probe (`AttentiveClassifier`) is the larger, frozen-only alternative: instead of one score per token, a single learnable query token attends over all 512 tokens through a full cross-attention block (6 heads of 384/64 for ViT-M), and the result passes through an MLP before the final classification layer. This gives it considerably more trainable weights than the §4.2 head, which is why it is the more forgiving of the two frozen heads for the released checkpoint, and why, in Experiment 4 below, we also try our own even simpler linear probe on the same frozen features, to check that neither of the authors' heads is itself the bottleneck [1].

Experiment 2 — Fine-tuning vs. training from scratch.
If the released weights are collapsed, does starting from them still help once the whole network is fine-tuned end-to-end, rather than kept frozen? The paper reports 0.885 AUROC after full fine-tuning. We fine-tuned the whole ViT-M plus §4.2 head twice (once from the released weights, once from random weights) on the same five training subsets, using the authors' own recipe (500 epochs, batch size 2, learning rate 1e-3, cosine decay). Batch 2 looked worth increasing for speed, so we tried 256 once on a protocol check before committing: it under-trains badly enough (59.7 AUROC) that the slower batch 2 won without a second thought. A “training subset” here, and in every TUAB experiment below, is one of five separate samples drawn from TUAB's 2,717-recording training pool (2,993 total minus the 276-recording eval set from Experiment 1), mirroring the paper's own five-subset protocol, whose exact recording membership the authors never published (§5.4).

Experiment 3 — Confirming representation collapse.
Experiments 1 and 2 both point the same way, so we tested it directly. For every recording, Maruf measured token standard deviation (how much the encoder's output varies within one recording) and the mean cosine similarity between every pair of recordings' embeddings, where 1.0 would mean every recording produces an identical output. Operationally (code/tuab_collapse_check.py), each recording contributes one deterministic clip (32 frames spread over its first 96), which the encoder turns into 512 tokens of 384 dimensions; token std is the standard deviation over those 512 tokens, taken per dimension and averaged over the 384 dimensions, and the cosine is computed between recordings' mean-pooled 384-dimensional embeddings and averaged over every pair of recordings.

The two groups don't overlap at all. The largest token std measured for the released checkpoint, on any recording, was 0.0085. The smallest measured for a random-weight twin was 0.379. That rules out measurement noise. The released EEG-VJEPA checkpoints give almost the same output for every input, on two entirely different patient populations. No preprocessing variation we tested changes this, and it holds on both datasets, so it appears to be a property of the released weights rather than of our pipeline or of which dataset we test on. We even checked whether our own z-scoring convention could be doing this: per-recording and per-window normalisation give the same collapsed number (token std 0.0059, cosine 1.0000) on the same 120 recordings. We fixed no numeric collapse threshold in advance: the criterion is separation from every random-weight twin, and a gap of 0.0085 against 0.379 needs no threshold to read. Token spread is the deciding measure. Cosine alone is not: on the 15-subject stroke cohort even random weights give a cross-recording cosine of 0.9991 (Table 9).
That raised an obvious question: why would collapse even be possible, given that the architecture has an anti-collapse safeguard built in? Their public implementation includes a variance-hinge term, `relu(1 - std)`, that penalises the predictor's output for having too little spread across the masked patches of a clip — the same kind of safeguard that motivated FEI's own BYOL-style design. Maruf went looking in their published training configs and found a likely explanation: the exact config file that produced the released ViT-M checkpoint (`configs/pretrain/cluster_vitl16_EEG.yaml`) sets that term's weight, `reg_coeff`, to 0.0. Switched off. What's left is a plain, unnormalised L1 loss between the predictor's output and a layer-normalised target, and a plain L1 loss can be satisfied almost as cheaply by a near-constant output as by a genuinely informative one. We have not re-run their pre-training with this term switched back on, so this is the most likely explanation we found in their own code and config, not an independently re-verified causal test. What is demonstrated is the collapse itself (Table 9) and the setting in the released config; that the setting caused the collapse is a hypothesis. A second, also untested, candidate sits in the same code: pre-training saves `jepa-best`, the checkpoint with the lowest training loss, and the released file is that checkpoint (epoch 353). Under a loss that a near-constant output can satisfy, the lowest-loss checkpoint may well be the most collapsed one.

Experiment 7 — Pipeline-soundness gate and the clinical BSI baseline (stroke).
Before trusting any model's number on the small, 15-subject stroke cohort, Soumya first checked that the data pipeline reproduces a well-established classical effect: stroke patients show higher left-right power asymmetry (a higher Brain Symmetry Index) than healthy controls. This gate was pre-declared, with a fixed significance threshold, before anyone looked at a single model's classification result (it is the gate section of runs/act0/phaseB/PREREGISTRATION.md, written on 2026-09-25 at 23:55, UTC+6; Appendix §11.2).
Concretely, Soumya implemented the pairwise-derived BSI (pdBSI) of Sheorajpanday et al. (2009) [22], a more localized variant than the single whole-head ratio of the original BSI cited above (van Putten & Tavy, 2004 [9]): for each of six symmetric electrode pairs (FC1/FC2, FC3/FC4, C1/C2, C3/C4, C5/C6, CP1/CP2) and every frequency bin in the band of interest, pdBSI = mean( |(P_R − P_L) / (P_R + P_L)| ), where P_L and P_R are that pair's left- and right-hemisphere spectral power; the reported score averages this ratio across all six pairs and every bin. Taking the mean of six local ratios rather than one global ratio makes the measure more sensitive to a focal asymmetry — expected after a stroke restricted to one hemisphere — which is why it was the better choice for a cohort this small.

Experiment 8 — FEI+GTJ on stroke, after fixing a distribution-shift problem.
The first attempt to run FEI+GTJ on stroke data ran straight into a wrong assumption. We'd treated NMT and the stroke cohort as if they shared the same recording band, 0.5–40 Hz. They don't: NMT's own EDF header has a blank prefilter field, and its real spectrum rolls off closer to 35 Hz, while the stroke side's 0.5–40 Hz filter passes content up to about 44–45 Hz. Soumya traced this down after GTJ's BatchNorm layers, which store NMT's input statistics, saw the stroke cohort's spectrum as wildly out of range (z-scores of 4.3 standard deviations or more) and collapsed. Same failure mode as Experiment 3, this time self-inflicted by a spectrum mismatch rather than a training-objective problem.
The fix: a label-free, per-channel zero-phase filter that maps the stroke cohort's mean spectrum onto NMT's, gated on numerical thresholds. This step was not pre-registered: under the pre-registered input, FEI+GTJ had scored 0.833 LOSO AUROC, below its random-init twin's 0.889, and that result is what prompted the fix. The recipe, its gates and the comparisons to run were written to runs/act0/phaseB_clean/POSTHOC.md before any LOSO run on the corrected input (Appendix §11.2), and every gate passed. The largest gap between the stroke and NMT log-spectra fell from 5.81 to 0.18 decades (gate: ≤ 0.25 decades), GTJ's BatchNorm inputs fell from |z| ≈ 4.3 to ≈ 0.37, and no embedding dimension stayed constant across subjects. Experiment 8 is therefore a post-hoc repair of a distribution shift, tested on the same 15 subjects whose result prompted it, not an independent confirmation. Refitting the filter inside each fold (Table 19) rules out test-subject leakage, but the method itself was shaped by this cohort; whether it carries over needs a new cohort.

The released ViT-M (VJEPA) reaches 0.981 LOSO AUROC on this same cohort, higher than FEI+GTJ, FEI+GTJ+BSI, or BSI alone. But we don't read this as the released checkpoint working here. On this 15-subject cohort it is just as collapsed as on TUAB (Table 9: token std 0.0060, cross-recording cosine 0.999994; each of its output features varies across the 15 subjects by only 0.0014 on average, against about 0.018 for a random-weight twin), so a linear probe is separating cases on a vanishing residual that feature-scaling blows back up, a residual that could just as easily track a recording-condition difference between the stroke and control sessions as track stroke itself. Consistent with that, the same checkpoint's score drops from 0.981 on this cohort to 0.863 on the broader sensitivity cohort (a superset of these 15 subjects: 10 stroke vs. 8 controls, first 300 s rather than the 285 s rest block): the kind of instability a collapsed representation produces, rather than a genuine clinical signal.
5.3.4 Interpretation and Conclusions on Data
The released EEG-VJEPA checkpoints don't reproduce the paper's own result, because they're collapsed (Experiments 1–3). No amount of fine-tuning under the authors' own recipe fixes that (Experiment 2), and the simplest possible probe can't rescue it either (Experiment 4). FEI+GTJ, built in response, beats a classical baseline on the data it was trained on, although an untrained twin already accounts for about two-thirds of that margin (Experiment 5); on a different hospital's data, FEI's NMT-only pre-training still gives a significant gain, in all three pre-training runs, and the fused model, re-pre-trained with that hospital's unlabelled recordings, scores close to the released checkpoint (difference not significant) and stays ahead of a classical baseline (Experiment 6), and, after a post-hoc input fix, scores close to a strong clinical baseline on a third, structurally different task, too close for 15 subjects to separate (Experiments 7–8). A t-SNE/UMAP visual check, matching our original proposal's promised deliverable, shows that 2-D unsupervised layout is the wrong tool for seeing why any of these encoders work, or why the released one doesn't.
A second analysis pass, outside the original proposal, re-examined the per-recording predictions already saved from the experiments above, with no retraining. The question: when FEI and GTJ are wrong, are they wrong the same way, or differently, and does that explain where fusion helps and where it doesn't?

• Children under 18 make up 43% of NMT's recordings but 65% of its abnormal-labelled ones, and FEI flags 62% of healthy 0–6-year-olds as abnormal: by far its single largest source of false alarms, even though its adult performance is its strongest (age alone predicts the label with AUROC 0.609 on NMT, and FEI's scores correlate with age within the normal class at r = −0.42). FEI's main failure mode is age-dependent. Our working, untested interpretation is that FEI's pretext task (predicting how its target encoder embeds a window once random frequency bins are removed) makes it sensitive to slow-wave activity that is a normal feature of a healthy developing brain in young children, rather than a sign of pathology. This pattern cannot be checked on TUAB, which has only 2 recordings under 18.

• FEI is also weaker on adult women, though this is only a flag. On NMT adults, FEI reaches 0.773 AUROC on women vs. 0.864 on men; the gap narrows for GTJ (0.832 vs. 0.852) and for FEI+GTJ (0.846 vs. 0.883). The same direction appears on TUAB (FEI 0.865 on women vs. 0.883 on men). This isn't simply the age effect above in disguise, since it persists within children alone too (FEI 0.756 female vs. 0.767 male), but we have not run a significance test or identified a mechanism, so we report this as an observation for future work rather than a finding.

• On NMT, FEI and GTJ's per-recording errors overlap only modestly (score correlation 0.60), and fusion repairs 321 of FEI's 622 errors while introducing just 21 new ones. With the NMT-only encoders on TUAB, the two branches' scores instead correlate at 0.84 and 44 of FEI's 58 errors are also GTJ errors, so fusion has little left to fix: this NMT-only FEI+GTJ (0.857) falls slightly below FEI alone (0.872; paired difference −0.015, 95% CI [−0.047, +0.016], p = 0.36). Table 12's fused row (0.862) is the separately re-pre-trained joint version. The two branches also fail for different reasons: FEI's main failure mode is age (it flags 62% of healthy 0–6-year-olds on NMT as abnormal), while GTJ's main failures turn out to be distribution-shift failures, rather than age failures. Fusion, in short, only helps when its two inputs disagree, which explains both Experiment 5's strong fusion gain on NMT and Experiment 6's weaker one on TUAB.

• GTJ is confidently wrong roughly twice as often as FEI is. Looking only at each model's mistakes, FEI's tend to sit close to the 0.5 decision boundary (a clinically useful property: uncertain cases could be flagged for human review), while GTJ makes confident, large-margin mistakes about twice as often on NMT and more than seven times as often on TUAB. Because fusion inherits GTJ's confident errors, this is a calibration weakness specific to GTJ, separate from its overall ranking ability.

• A single global decision threshold is a poor fit across ages. At the standard 0.5 threshold, FEI+GTJ catches only 50–69% of abnormal recordings in adults while over-calling many healthy children's recordings as abnormal, because the classifier is trained on a population where most abnormal examples are children, so its decision boundary is implicitly tuned for them. The model's ranking (AUROC) is fine in adults; what fails is the single shared threshold.

• In an exploratory run, GTJ's NMT AUROC dropped in every one of 5 folds (−0.014) when pre-trained jointly on NMT plus unlabelled TUAB rather than NMT alone, and its embedding spread on NMT fell too, a sign that it reallocates representational capacity toward whichever domain it is shown, rather than learning more general structure. Feeding GTJ more data during pre-training, in this setup, made it worse. Consistent with this, GTJ's past-predicts-future pretext loss was observed to plateau within roughly 10 epochs, suggesting that for this architecture and this amount of data, the task is simply too easy to force deep, transferable learning.

• One Fig-3-head number is not reproducible even by us, on the same seed. Maruf re-ran the attention-head extraction behind the Fig-3 attentive probe (Figure 15) to double-check it before freezing the plots, and got 72.1 AUROC the first time and 68.7 on the exact re-run: same seed, same code, same checkpoint (Table 7 reports the re-run; with the first run its five-subset mean would read 0.720). The likely cause is GPU-level non-determinism in one of the attention kernels (cuDNN does not guarantee bit-identical output across runs unless you force deterministic algorithms, which we had not). We did not chase this down further; it is flagged here because it is a real gap in our own reproducibility as well, beyond the paper's. The §4.2 AttentionClassifier numbers are bit-identical across re-runs, and no conclusion in this report depends on the Fig-3 head's exact value.
The released EEG-VJEPA checkpoints are collapsed and fail to reproduce the paper; our token-spread diagnostic is repeatable, and the released training config gives a likely cause. FEI+GTJ beats our classical baseline on NMT, with pre-training adding a consistent but not significant +0.027 AUROC over an untrained twin (three-run mean 0.861 vs 0.834); on stroke, after a post-hoc input fix, its AUROC is close to BSI's, with far too few subjects to call them equivalent. Across hospitals, FEI's NMT-only pre-training still gives a significant gain, in all three pre-training runs; the fused model, re-pre-trained with TUAB's unlabelled recordings, scores close to the released ViT-M there, with no significant difference. On stroke only FEI shows a pre-training gain, and no model here significantly beats BSI. Table 16 compares these outcomes with the original proposal.

5.4 Limitations of Tools (PO(e))

• Compute. Everything in this report, pre-training and evaluation alike, ran on one shared consumer GPU (RTX 5060 Ti) behind a thermal guard that pauses or kills a job if the CPU gets too hot. That capped how many random-init seeds we could afford for some controls (3, not 10) and ruled out the much larger pre-training runs that the released EEG-VJEPA or models like CBraMod/LaBraM use. Appendix §11.5 lists the checks we did not run and why.

• Cohort size. The stroke cohort is 15 subjects. Every confidence interval there is wide, and some comparisons (FEI+GTJ vs. its random-init twin, for one) can't rule out even fairly large true differences in either direction.

• Demographic confound in the stroke cohort. Per the dataset's own record description [13], the stroke patients (55.9 ± 14.2 years, 6 male / 4 female) are older and more often male than the controls (46.3 ± 23.6 years, 1 male / 6 female); these figures describe the whole record, not exactly the 15 subjects we analyse. Encoders can read age and sex off EEG, so part of any stroke-vs-control score in Experiments 7–8 may just be reading those group differences back out. With 15 subjects there's no way to separate the two statistically: Soumya flagged this while building the cohort table, and we checked it couldn't be fixed by just re-splitting, since the imbalance is in who was recruited, not how we split them.

• Data access. Our copy of TUAB came from an unofficial mirror rather than the licensed channel (see Legal Issues, §5.5.3). That limits what we can responsibly do with those specific numbers.

• Unpublished details in the reference paper. The EEG-VJEPA paper doesn't say exactly which 546 recordings make up each of its five reported training subsets, which training epoch its headline numbers come from, or the exact fine-tuning recipe behind its 0.885 AUROC. We fixed our own choices with a seeded sampler (code/tuab_released_eval.py; the five 546-recording subsets are listed file by file in docs/act0_repro/tuab_subsets.csv), but there's no guarantee they match what the authors actually did.

• Software environment. Ordinary friction (recent Debian/Ubuntu Python blocks a direct `pip install` outside a virtual environment, that kind of thing) cost real hours of environment setup that have nothing to do with EEG. It was a genuine time sink, and none of it will ever show up in a related-work section. The environment every number here came from (Python 3.9.25, PyTorch 2.8.0 with CUDA 12.8, MNE 1.8.0, scikit-learn 1.5.2) is listed in Table 18, and every pinned package in docs/act0_repro/requirements-act0.txt.
5.5 Impact Assessment (PO(f))
5.5.1 Assessment of Societal and Cultural Issues
Our TUAB result (Experiment 6) shows that a self-supervised encoder pre-trained only on a lower-resource-setting EEG dataset (FEI, pre-trained on NMT) can carry a measurable benefit, zero-shot, to a different hospital's data.
5.5.2 Assessment of Health and Safety Issues
FEI's false-alarm rate in young children (§5.2.1), and the other demographic-specific failure modes in §5.3.4, mean FEI+GTJ should stay a research artefact for now: not a clinical decision-support tool, not without age-stratified validation and recalibration first.
5.5.3 Assessment of Legal Issues
The official TUH Abnormal EEG Corpus is distributed by Temple University under a Data Use Agreement (DUA) signed with the corpus's maintainers (NEDC) before access is granted. The copy we used came from an unofficial, publicly re-hosted Kaggle mirror: we checked it matches the structure and content of the genuine TUAB v3.0.1 release, but it is not covered by that licence. So every TUAB-derived number in this report (Experiments 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and the visual check) is presented for internal academic evaluation within this course submission only, and should not be published, redistributed, or reused outside it. Any future public-facing use — a paper, a public leaderboard entry, a public repository result — would need either a properly licensed copy of TUAB through the official DUA process, or would need to drop the TUAB numbers and rely only on NMT and the openly-licensed stroke dataset.
5.6 Sustainability Evaluation (PO(g))
Small models and thermal-guarded, single-GPU training also limit compute demand (§5.2.2). CBraMod and LaBraM [5, 6] pre-train on tens of thousands of hours, while our two encoders have 1.15M (FEI) and 1.88M (GTJ) parameters, online and target copies included (Tables 1–2), and were trained for 60 epochs on a few thousand recordings. It still clears a classical baseline and, once TUAB's unlabelled recordings are added to pre-training, scores close to a released large-scale checkpoint's linear-probe score on TUAB, with no significant difference. If that holds more broadly, EEG models could be sized to the data available in a clinical setting, often small and expensive to collect, instead of defaulting to the biggest architecture on the shelf.
5.7 Ethical Issues (PO(h))
All three datasets were collected, de-identified, and released by their original curators under their own ethical review and consent processes; we are secondary users of already-public, de-identified data and collected none ourselves. We disclosed that our TUAB copy came from an unofficial source (§5.5.3), limited its numbers to this internal submission, and stated what proper licensing would require. A model with an undisclosed, demographic-specific failure mode (FEI over-flagging healthy young children at six times the adult rate) could do real harm if someone mistook it for a finished clinical tool instead of a term project.

7 Communication to External Stakeholders (PO(j))
7.1 Executive Summary
A recently published AI model, EEG-VJEPA, claimed to automatically tell healthy brain recordings apart from abnormal ones by adapting a video-understanding technique to EEG. Our team tested this claim and found that the publicly released version does not actually work as described: it gives almost the same answer for every patient. We could show this directly. We built two smaller models instead: one for frequency content and one for how a signal's rhythm changes over time. Together they beat a simple classical measurement on the dataset they were trained on (an untrained copy of the same design already does most of that) and score close to an established clinical measurement, with too few patients to tell the two apart, on a third task: telling stroke patients apart from healthy volunteers. On a different hospital's data, the frequency model's training still helped on its own; the combined model needed that hospital's unlabelled recordings too, and there it only scored close to the released model.
7.2 Reproducing Our Results
All code, saved model checkpoints, and saved per-recording predictions used to produce every number, table, and figure in this report are organized as a set of scripts, one per experiment (Table 5). Table 4 gives the script behind each experiment's input. The 200 Hz NMT copy that FEI and GTJ read (per-channel z-scored at NMT's native rate, stored as 2.5-second frames) was first written in August 2026 by a script that was not kept; we reconstructed its recipe as code/preprocess_nmt_200hz.py, which reproduces all 2,417 stored files byte for byte. From those inputs, pre-train an encoder (`fei_pretrain.py` or `fei_branchC.py`, both with a `--selftest` flag for a fast sanity check with no real data and a `--cv` flag for the full five-fold cross-validation reported here), score it with `feic_nmt_anchor.py` (Experiment 5) or `tuab_feic.py` (Experiment 6), and evaluate a frozen or fine-tuned released checkpoint with the corresponding `tuab_*_eval.py` script. Every long-running script checkpoints its progress after each cross-validation fold and can be safely re-run to resume after an interruption, and each accepts command-line flags for every experiment variant reported here. The code is in github.com/mashfiqurrahman2020-coder/EEG-VJEPA-act-0; its docs/act0_repro/ folder holds the fold, subset and cohort lists, the SHA-256 of every checkpoint and result file, and the pinned environment (Appendix §11). The code, checkpoints and results behind this report are commit e0d6d64ab57f0da9dd32e807a36be5a23fa5a808, which unlike a tag cannot be moved: git clone https://github.com/mashfiqurrahman2020-coder/EEG-VJEPA-act-0.git, then git checkout e0d6d64ab57f, then pip install -r docs/act0_repro/requirements-act0.txt under Python 3.9. Appendix §11.6 gives the pre-training commands and §11.9 the data each experiment uses. The report itself is added one commit later, under the tag act0-report-round15b. All 40 FEI/GTJ checkpoints are committed in the repository (code/*.pt, about 110 MB), so nothing needs a separate download except the data and the released EEG-VJEPA weights. Two kinds of reproduction follow. The saved checkpoints and predictions reproduce every reported number exactly. Re-training does not: the original FEI and GTJ runs set no random seed, so they cannot be regenerated bit for bit, and cuDNN kernels were left non-deterministic, so even the seeded re-runs should be expected to reproduce the results statistically, not bit for bit.
8 Project Management and Cost Analysis (PO(k))
8.1 Computational Resources Used
This software project has no physical bill of materials. Compute was the resource budget. All pre-training and evaluation ran on a locally-owned workstation with one consumer-grade GPU (NVIDIA RTX 5060 Ti); we used no paid cloud compute. The direct monetary cost was the workstation's electricity. The practical cost that mattered day to day was time and thermal headroom: every script in this project is gated by a shared hardware guard that pauses training if CPU temperature rises above a safe threshold, which shaped how many experiments (and how many random-init control seeds) we could fit into the term.
To put a concrete number on it: `code/runs/act0/feic_joint.log` records the 5-fold cross-validation run that pre-trains FEI (1.15M parameters, Table 1) and GTJ (1.88M parameters, Table 2) — each independently, not against a shared loss — on the joint NMT+TUAB training pool, the "joint-pretraining" data variant compared against this report's main NMT-only numbers elsewhere. It trains 10 encoders in total (one FEI and one GTJ per fold), each for 60 epochs of 145 steps. Per-epoch time, sampled every 10th epoch across all 10 runs, ranged from 2 s to 24 s (FEI averaging ≈5.7 s/epoch; GTJ averaging ≈9.8 s/epoch, roughly double, reflecting its larger encoder and sequential GRU), with no systematic drift across epochs — the spread is scheduling noise from the shared GPU and the thermal guard, not a slowdown over time. Extrapolating each run's sampled mean to its full 60 epochs puts the entire 10-run job at approximately 78 minutes of GPU time; this is an estimate (only every 10th epoch is timestamped in the log), not an exact sum.

9 Future Work (PO(l))

• Age-aware modelling. FEI's false-alarm rate in healthy young children is far higher than in adults (§5.3.4), the clearest practical finding in this report. An age-conditioned probe, and AUROC reported by age band as standard practice, should follow.

• A general fix for GTJ. The spectrum-matching fix in Experiment 8 was built for the stroke cohort specifically; a dataset-agnostic version (replacing BatchNorm with per-recording normalisation, say) could let GTJ transfer the way FEI already does. The same work should cover GTJ's confidently-wrong errors (§5.3.4) too: a calibration step, such as temperature scaling fit on a held-out fold, before its output is read as a probability rather than a ranking. Before any of that, the spectral-matching recipe should be frozen and applied unchanged to a new stroke cohort: Experiment 8 only shows it repairing the cohort it was designed on.

• A properly licensed TUAB evaluation. Obtaining the official, DUA-covered TUH Abnormal EEG Corpus would let the TUAB-derived findings here be verified, extended, and published without the internal-only restriction in §5.5.3.

• Motor imagery. The team has already started a separate extension of this work into motor-imagery brain-computer interfaces, building on GTJ's spectral-dynamics design, out of scope for this report.

11.6 Pre-training Configuration and Commands
Every encoder pre-trained for this report uses the recipe below. None of the runs uses a learning-rate schedule, mixed precision, gradient clipping or PyTorch's deterministic-algorithm flags, and each keeps its final (60th) epoch's weights, with no checkpoint selection. cuDNN kernels were left non-deterministic, so even the seeded runs are not bit-identical on a re-run. Random-init twins are built from the same architectures with torch seed = twin index (seeds 0–2 on TUAB and stroke, 0–4 on NMT).

11.8 Stroke Spectral-Matching Filter
Experiment 8's input is built by code/stroke_phaseB_clean.py, following runs/act0/phaseB_clean/POSTHOC.md, which was written before any LOSO run on this input. Each recording gets the dataset authors' bad-channel interpolation, a common average reference, the 19 NMT channels (T7/T8/P7/P8 renamed T3/T4/T5/T6), resampling to 200 Hz with no band-pass, and a per-channel z-score. The target is NMT's mean log10 Welch spectrum over 100 fixed-random NMT training recordings (first 300 s; 800-sample Welch segments, i.e. 0.25 Hz bins; 10⁻²⁰ added before the log).
The filter is a log10 amplitude gain per channel on that 0.25 Hz grid. It starts at zero and is updated four times: shape every subject, z-score again, average the cohort's log-spectra, and add half the remaining gap to NMT's. Shaping multiplies the recording's FFT by the gain, linearly interpolated onto the FFT's frequency grid with no smoothing; the gain is real, so the filter changes no phase (zero-phase). Each recording is reflect-padded by 800 samples at both ends before the FFT and trimmed after it. The result is z-scored again and cut into non-overlapping 2.5 s frames.
One filter per channel is fitted on all 15 subjects pooled, without labels, and the same filter is applied to every subject (Experiment 8); Table 19 refits it inside each LOSO fold on the 14 training subjects. Four gates were fixed in POSTHOC.md and reported whether or not they passed: the matched cohort's mean log-spectrum within 0.25 decades of NMT's at every channel and bin from 0.5 to 99 Hz; GTJ's input BatchNorm |z| at most 1.5 in every 1 Hz bin, against each NMT checkpoint's running statistics; GTJ's across-subject embedding spread at least half its spread on 15 NMT evaluation recordings; and at most 5% of its embedding dimensions constant (std < 10⁻⁵). All four passed (§5.3.3).
